LCM & Prime Factorisation

Number Theory · Week 5

Warm-Up

First — what is a multiple?

A multiple of a number is what you get when you multiply it by 1, 2, 3, 4, … and keep going forever.

Think of multiples like the times table for that number, just extended:

×1, ×2…
4
8
12
16
20
24
…

Those are the multiples of 4 — every number that 4 divides into exactly.

Now, two different numbers will sometimes share a multiple. The smallest one they share is called the LCM — Lowest Common Multiple.

LCM = the smallest number that both numbers divide into exactly.

Number Theory Key Fact

Primes — the building blocks of all numbers

A prime number has exactly two factors: 1 and itself. It can't be broken down further.

5

1 is NOT a prime — it only has one factor (itself), not two.

Every number can be written as a product of primes. This is called its prime factorisation.

Example: 12 = 2 × 2 × 3  (or 2² × 3)

Method 2 — Factor Tree

Prime factorisation with a factor tree

A factor tree breaks a number down step by step until every branch ends in a prime.

Example: Break down 12

12 / \ 4 3 ← prime ✓ / \ 2 2 ← both prime ✓ 12 = 2 × 2 × 3

Example: Break down 18

18 / \ 6 3 ← prime ✓ / \ 2 3 ← both prime ✓ 18 = 2 × 3 × 3
Keep splitting until every branch is a prime. The primes at the ends are the answer.

Method 2 — LCM via Prime Factorisation

The fast way to find LCM

Listing works fine for small numbers, but for bigger numbers (like 12 and 18) a factor tree is much faster. Here's the method:

1
Write the prime factorisation of each number.
2
For each prime, take the highest power that appears in either number.
3
Multiply those highest powers together.

Example: LCM(12, 18)

12 = 2² × 3¹ 18 = 2¹ × 3² Highest power of 2 → 2² = 4 Highest power of 3 → 3² = 9 LCM = 4 × 9 = 36
LCM(12, 18) = 36. Check: 36 ÷ 12 = 3 ✓   36 ÷ 18 = 2 ✓

Word Problems

When does LCM appear in real life?

LCM shows up whenever two things happen on different cycles and you need to find when they meet again at the same time.

Signal phrases: "at the same time again", "both happen together", "next time they meet", "after every X and every Y"

Classic example: Bus A comes every 4 minutes. Bus B comes every 6 minutes. They just left together. When do they next leave at the same time?

LCM(4, 6) = 12. Answer: in 12 minutes.

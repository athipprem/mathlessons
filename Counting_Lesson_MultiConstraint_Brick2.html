Brick 2 of 3

🎯 Why Learn This?

Sometimes there's more than one way to break the rule

In Brick 1, "at least 1 girl" was broken exactly ONE way — zero girls. But what about a rule like “not all the same colour”? If there are 3 colours, there are 3 different ways to be ALL the same colour — all red, all blue, OR all green. That's THREE bad cases, not one.

This is Brick 2 of 3. Today you learn what to do when BAD has more than one part — and a sneaky edge case where one of those parts is actually impossible.

🔁 First, Let’s Remind Ourselves — Total Minus Bad

Quick recap of Brick 1's rule, this time with a fresh example: fruit, not people. Say WHY each number is there as you read.

A basket has 9 pieces of fruit: 6 apples and 3 oranges. A bag of 3 pieces of fruit is chosen. How many different bags have AT LEAST 1 orange?

Step 1 — ALL (ignore the rule)
1
9 × 8 × 7 ÷ 6
9 is the whole basket (n=9). 8 and 7 count down (n−1, n−2). Divide by 6 to remove repeat-orders, same as always.
9×8=72, 72×7=504, 504÷6=84 (this is ALL)
Step 2 — BAD (the ONE way to break "at least 1 orange" = zero oranges = all apples)
2
6 × 5 × 4 ÷ 6
6 is now the apples-only total (we switched pools — only apples count as bad). 5 and 4 count down. Divide by 6 again to remove repeat-orders.
6×5=30, 30×4=120, 120÷6=20 (this is BAD)
Step 3 — Subtract
✓
84 − 20
ALL minus BAD leaves GOOD — exactly Brick 1's rule.
64 bags with at least 1 orange
GOOD = ALL − BAD  (Brick 1's rule — still true, still your starting point)

💡 The New Idea — Add Up EVERY Bad Case

A jar has 12 counters: 5 red, 4 blue, 3 green (12 in total). A handful of 3 counters is picked. How many different handfuls are NOT ALL THE SAME COLOUR?

What does "not all the same colour" break?

It's broken by picking 3 of the SAME colour. But now there are 3 different colours — so there are 3 separate ways to be bad: all 3 red, OR all 3 blue, OR all 3 green. You must find EACH one, then ADD them together.

Step 1 — ALL (ignore the rule)
1
12 × 11 × 10 ÷ 6
12 is the whole jar (n=12, all colours together). 11 and 10 count down. Divide by 6 to remove repeat-orders.
12×11=132, 132×10=1320, 1320÷6=220 (this is ALL)
Step 2 — BAD, case by case
a
5 × 4 × 3 ÷ 6
All-red bad case: 5 is the red-only total (n=5, we switch pools to just red). 4,3 count down. Divide by 6, same reason as always.
5×4=20, 20×3=60, 60÷6=10 all-red handfuls
b
4 × 3 × 2 ÷ 6
All-blue bad case: 4 is the blue-only total (n=4). 3,2 count down. Divide by 6.
4×3=12, 12×2=24, 24÷6=4 all-blue handfuls
c
3 × 2 × 1 ÷ 6
All-green bad case: 3 is the green-only total (n=3 — there are exactly 3 green, so there's only ONE way to pick all 3 of them). 2,1 count down. Divide by 6.
3×2=6, 6×1=6, 6÷6=1 all-green handful
Step 3 — ADD every bad case together, THEN subtract
✓
(10 + 4 + 1) then 220 − 15
First add all 3 bad cases: 10 (red) + 4 (blue) + 1 (green) = 15 total bad handfuls. THEN subtract that one combined BAD number from ALL — you only subtract once, using the sum.
220 − 15 = 205 handfuls that are NOT all the same colour
More than one way to be bad?  Find EACH bad case  →  ADD them  →  THEN subtract the total from ALL.

⚠️ A Sneaky Trap — When a Bad Case is IMPOSSIBLE

A jar has 11 counters: 2 red, 5 blue, 4 green (11 in total). A handful of 3 is picked. How many different handfuls are NOT ALL THE SAME COLOUR?

Look closely at the red count

There are only 2 red counters. You cannot pick 3 red counters if only 2 exist! The "all-red" bad case is impossible — so its count is 0, not skipped, not left out. You still write it down as 0 and add it in.

Step 1 — ALL
1
11 × 10 × 9 ÷ 6
11 is the whole jar (n=11). 10,9 count down. Divide by 6.
11×10=110, 110×9=990, 990÷6=165 (this is ALL)
Step 2 — BAD, case by case (watch the red one!)
a
0  (cannot choose 3 from only 2)
All-red bad case: there are only 2 red counters, and you need 3. Whenever the group size is SMALLER than 3, the bad count for that group is always 0 — write it down, don't skip it.
All-red = 0 handfuls (impossible)
b
5 × 4 × 3 ÷ 6
All-blue bad case: 5 is the blue-only total (n=5). 4,3 count down. Divide by 6.
5×4=20, 20×3=60, 60÷6=10 all-blue handfuls
c
4 × 3 × 2 ÷ 6
All-green bad case: 4 is the green-only total (n=4). 3,2 count down. Divide by 6.
4×3=12, 12×2=24, 24÷6=4 all-green handfuls
Step 3 — ADD (including the 0!), THEN subtract
✓
(0 + 10 + 4) then 165 − 14
0 (red) + 10 (blue) + 4 (green) = 14 total bad. The 0 still counts as a term in the sum — leaving it out would give the wrong total.
165 − 14 = 151 handfuls that are NOT all the same colour

⚠️ The rule: if a colour (or group) has FEWER than 3 members, its bad case is 0 — you literally cannot choose 3 from fewer than 3. Write the 0 down and keep adding.

🔍 Watch Out — "Not All Same" vs "At Least 1"

These two rules LOOK similar but need different BAD counts. Same jar, two different questions:

A jar has 20 buttons: 9 red, 6 blue, 5 green. A handful of 3 is picked.

Question A — "NOT ALL THE SAME colour"

Broken by all-red OR all-blue OR all-green — 3 bad cases, add them.

BAD = C(9,3)+C(6,3)+C(5,3) = 84+20+10 = 114
GOOD = 1140 − 114 = 1026

Question B — "AT LEAST 1 blue"

Broken only by ZERO blue — 1 bad case (choose 3 from the 14 non-blue buttons).

BAD = C(14,3) = 364
GOOD = 1140 − 364 = 776

⚠️ The test: "at least 1 [group]" → only ONE bad case (zero of that one group). "Not all the same" → ONE bad case PER group (all-red, all-blue, all-green…), added together. Read the question slowly and ask: how many DIFFERENT ways can this rule be broken?

⭐ Your Turn

Practice — 4 Questions

🚦 For each question, first count HOW MANY separate bad cases there are, before you calculate anything.

P1.  A bag has 10 marbles: 4 red, 3 blue, 3 green. A handful of 3 is picked. How many handfuls are NOT ALL THE SAME COLOUR?

ALL: choose 3 from 10 → 10×9×8=720, 720÷6=120.
BAD: all-red C(4,3)=4, all-blue C(3,3)=1, all-green C(3,3)=1 → 4+1+1=6.
GOOD: 120−6=114 handfuls.
A) 120 is ALL, B) 6 is BAD — both half-finished.

P2.  A drawer has 13 keys: 2 gold, 6 silver, 5 bronze. A handful of 3 is picked. How many handfuls are NOT ALL THE SAME COLOUR?

ALL: choose 3 from 13 → 13×12×11=1716, 1716÷6=286.
BAD: all-gold — only 2 gold keys exist, so C(2,3)=0 (write it down!). all-silver C(6,3)=20. all-bronze C(5,3)=10. Sum: 0+20+10=30.
GOOD: 286−30=256 handfuls.

P3.  The SAME drawer as P2 (2 gold, 6 silver, 5 bronze, 13 in total) is picked again, but this time the rule is AT LEAST 1 GOLD. How many handfuls of 3 have at least 1 gold key?

"At least 1 gold" is Brick 1's shape — only ONE bad case (zero gold = all from the 11 non-gold keys).
ALL=286. BAD=C(11,3)=11×10×9÷6=165.
GOOD: 286−165=121.
A) 256 is the answer to a DIFFERENT question (P2's "not all same") — don't mix them up.

P4.  A pencil case has 15 pens: 6 blue, 5 black, 4 red. A handful of 3 is picked. How many handfuls are NOT ALL THE SAME COLOUR?

ALL: choose 3 from 15 → 15×14×13=2730, 2730÷6=455.
BAD: all-blue C(6,3)=20, all-black C(5,3)=10, all-red C(4,3)=4 → 20+10+4=34.
GOOD: 455−34=421 handfuls.

📖 Brick 2's Big Idea

When a rule can be broken in MORE than one way, find EACH bad case separately, ADD them all together (even a 0!), then subtract that ONE combined number from ALL.

GOOD = ALL − (BAD case 1 + BAD case 2 + BAD case 3 + …)

🚦 Finish-Line Check — count how many separate bad cases the question describes BEFORE you calculate. If a group has fewer than 3 members, its bad case is 0 — write it down, don't skip it.

🏆 You've got it when…

  • You can spot how many separate bad cases a "not all the same" rule creates
  • You find each bad case with its own Choose-3 calculation, then ADD them
  • You know a group smaller than 3 gives a bad case of 0 — and you still write it down
  • You can tell "not all the same" (many bad cases) apart from "at least 1" (one bad case)

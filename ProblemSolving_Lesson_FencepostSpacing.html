🌳 Why This Matters

Real life is full of "spacing" problems

Planting trees along a road, putting up streetlights, placing bus stops, hammering in fence posts, hanging party lights around a garden — all of these ask the SAME hidden question: "if I know the total distance and the gap between each item, how many items do I need?" Get one small detail wrong — whether the ends are open or joined into a loop — and your count is off.

The famous trap

Imagine a 20-metre fence with a post every 5 metres. It's tempting to say "20 ÷ 5 = 4 posts." But picture it: post—gap—post—gap—post—gap—post—gap—post. That's 5 posts, not 4! There's always ONE MORE post than there are gaps, on a straight line. This is called a "fencepost" problem, and today you'll learn exactly when to add that extra +1 — and when NOT to.

Straight line with two open ends → posts = gaps + 1. Closed loop → no +1 needed!

See It For Yourself

Tap "Add a gap" a few times and watch what happens to the post count.

Gaps: 0  |  Posts: 1

⚠️ Notice: every time you add ONE gap, you add ONE post — but you started with 1 post already there before any gaps existed. That's where the "+1" comes from.

The Formula — Straight, Open-Ended Line

posts = (total length ÷ spacing) + 1

A straight path is 24 metres long. A lamp post is placed every 4 metres, starting at one end and ending at the other end. How many lamp posts are needed?

Step 1 — find the number of GAPS: 24 ÷ 4 = 6 gaps
Step 2 — add 1 for the starting post: 6 + 1 = 7
7 lamp posts

⚠️ This +1 rule only applies when BOTH ends are open and BOTH ends get a post (like a straight fence, a row of trees along a road, or lamp posts on a path).

🔄 The Twist — Closed Loops Don't Need +1

Remember from the last lesson: a circle doesn't have a fixed "start" or "end" — it just loops around. The exact same idea applies here!

Closed loop: items = total distance ÷ spacing  (NO +1)

A circular pond has a circumference (distance all the way around) of 24 metres. Lily pad decorations are placed every 4 metres around the edge. How many lily pads are needed?

Step 1 — find the number of gaps: 24 ÷ 4 = 6 gaps
Step 2 — it's a LOOP, so the last gap connects straight back to the first lily pad — there's no extra "end" that needs its own decoration.
6 lily pads (NOT 7)

⚠️ Same 24 metres, same 4-metre spacing as the lamp post example — but the loop answer is ONE LESS than the straight-line answer. That missing +1 is the whole trick.

The "Maximum Count" Twist

Sarah wants to plant flowers along a straight 28-metre path. Each flower needs at least 7 metres of space from the next one. Including both ends of the path, what is the MAXIMUM number of flowers she can plant?

Step 1 — "at least 7 metres apart" means use EXACTLY 7 metres to fit the most flowers: 28 ÷ 7 = 4 gaps
Step 2 — straight path, both ends included → add 1: 4 + 1 = 5
5 flowers

⚠️ "Maximum number" with a "minimum spacing" rule just means: use the SMALLEST legal spacing to fit the MOST items. The +1 rule for straight lines still applies exactly the same way.

🌿 Going 2D — Rows AND Columns

A rectangular garden is 18 metres wide and 24 metres long. Trees will be planted in a full grid pattern, at least 6 metres apart in each direction, including all edges. What is the maximum number of trees that can be planted?

Do EACH direction separately with the straight-line rule, then multiply
Step 1 — rows across the width: 18 ÷ 6 + 1 = 3 + 1 = 4 rows
Step 2 — columns across the length: 24 ÷ 6 + 1 = 4 + 1 = 5 columns
Step 3 — total trees in the grid: 4 × 5 = 20
20 trees

⚠️ Each direction of a rectangle is its own straight line — both edges get a tree, so both directions use the +1 rule before you multiply them together.

🗺️ The Fencepost Decision Tree

1Find the number of GAPS first: total distance ÷ spacing.
2Is it a STRAIGHT LINE with two separate, open ends? → ADD 1 to the gap count.
3Is it a CLOSED LOOP (circle, round track, round pond)? → do NOT add 1. Gaps = items.
4Is it a RECTANGLE / 2D grid? → apply Steps 1–2 separately to width and length, then MULTIPLY the two counts.
5"Maximum items, minimum spacing"? → use the smallest legal spacing value, then apply Steps 1–4 as normal.

Practice — Use The Decision Tree

For each one: straight line, loop, or grid? Does it need the +1?

P1.  A straight fence is 36 metres long. A post is placed every 6 metres, including both ends. How many posts are needed?
Straight, both ends: 36÷6=6 gaps, +1 = 7
7
P2.  A circular running track has a distance of 36 metres around it. Flags are placed every 6 metres around the loop. How many flags are needed?
Loop, no +1: 36÷6=6 gaps = 6 flags
6
P3.  A straight garden bed is 50 metres long. Each plant needs at least 10 metres of space. Including both ends, what is the maximum number of plants?
Straight, both ends: 50÷10=5 gaps, +1 = 6
6
P4.  A rectangular field is 12 metres wide and 20 metres long. Trees are planted in a full grid, at least 4 metres apart in each direction, including all edges. What is the maximum number of trees?
Rows: 12÷4+1=4. Cols: 20÷4+1=6.
4 × 6 = 24
24
📖 Today's Rule

Straight line, both ends open → gaps + 1. Closed loop → no +1. Rectangle → do each side separately, then multiply.

🏆 You've got it when…

  • You always ask "does this loop, or does it have two separate ends?" before doing any dividing
  • You never forget the +1 on a straight line question
  • You never ADD a +1 on a closed-loop question
  • For a rectangle, you handle width and length separately before multiplying

This is the exact same "boundary" thinking from Lesson 1's circles — you now have it working across TWO different topics!

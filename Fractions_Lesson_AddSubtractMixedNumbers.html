🎯 Why Learn This?

You already meet mixed numbers all the time

A recipe needs 2½ cups of flour. A plank of wood is 1¾ metres long. You have 3⅓ hours until your favourite show starts. All of these are mixed numbers — a whole number PLUS a fraction, stuck together. Cooks, builders, and scientists add and subtract them constantly: "how much flour do I have left?", "how much wood do I need to cut off?"

Your new superpower

By the end of this lesson, you'll be able to add and subtract ANY two mixed numbers — even when their fraction parts don't match, and even when subtracting means you have to "borrow" a whole. No more guessing — a clean method that works every time.

Mixed number = a whole number + a fraction, glued together.

🔄 Quick Refresher — Mixed ↔ Improper

You've met this before, so let's warm up fast. A mixed number like 2¾ means 2 wholes and ¾ of another one. Sometimes it's easier to write it as ONE fraction instead — an improper fraction (top number bigger than the bottom).

Mixed → improper: multiply the whole by the denominator, ADD the numerator, keep the same denominator.
2¾ → (2×4)+3 = 11  →  11/4
Improper → mixed: divide top by bottom. The answer is the whole number, the REMAINDER becomes the new numerator.
11/4 → 11 ÷ 4 = 2 remainder 3  →  2¾

⚠️ You'll use BOTH directions in this lesson — improper fractions make adding/subtracting easier to think about, but your final answer should usually be written back as a mixed number.

➕ Adding Mixed Numbers

The one new idea

To add two mixed numbers: add the WHOLE parts together, add the FRACTION parts together, then combine. The only catch — the fraction parts must have the SAME denominator before you can add them. If they don't match, find a common denominator first (a number both denominators divide into evenly), just like you already do when comparing fractions.

1Denominators match? If not, find a common denominator and convert both fractions first.
2Add the WHOLE numbers together.
3Add the FRACTION numerators together (denominator stays the same).
4If the fraction part is improper (top ≥ bottom), convert the extra into another whole.

⚠️ Step 1 is the part that's new — everything after it, you already know how to do.

Worked Example — Different Denominators

2⅓ + 1¼ = ?

Step 1 — denominators are 3 and 4. They don't match, so find a common denominator. 3 and 4 both divide evenly into 12 (3×4=12), so 12 is our common denominator:
2⅓ = 2 412  (⅓ × 4/4 = 4/12)
1¼ = 1 312  (¼ × 3/3 = 3/12)
Step 2 — add the wholes:
2 + 1 = 3
Step 3 — add the fraction numerators (denominator stays 12):
4/12 + 3/12 = 7/12
Step 4 — is 7/12 improper? No (7 < 12), so nothing to carry over. Combine:
2⅓ + 1¼ = 3 7/12

📋 Build The Sum Yourself

1½ + 2¼ = ?  Tap each button below in order to work through it.

Denominators are 2 and 4. 2 divides evenly into 4, so 4 is the common denominator. 1½ = 1 2/4 (½ × 2/2 = 2/4). 2¼ already has denominator 4.
1 + 2 = 3
2/4 + 1/4 = 3/4
3/4 is already in simplest form (3 and 4 share no common factor). 1½ + 2¼ = 3¾

➖ Subtracting Mixed Numbers

Same idea, one extra check

Subtracting works the same way as adding: match denominators first, then subtract the wholes and subtract the fractions separately. There's ONE new thing to watch for, which the next screen covers — but let's see the straightforward case first.

5¾ − 2¼ = ?

Step 1 — denominators already match (both 4).
Step 2 — subtract the wholes:
5 − 2 = 3
Step 3 — subtract the fractions (top number is bigger, so this is easy):
¾ − ¼ = 2/4
Step 4 — simplify:
5¾ − 2¼ = 3 2/4 = 3½

⚠️ This worked cleanly because ¾ is bigger than ¼ — the fraction you're taking away fits inside the fraction you started with. Next screen: what happens when it DOESN'T fit.

🍞 When The Fraction Doesn't Fit — Borrowing

The one new idea

What if the fraction you're subtracting is BIGGER than the fraction you're starting with? You can't take more slices of pizza than you have! The fix: borrow 1 whole from the whole-number part, and turn that borrowed whole into extra fraction pieces (denominator-many of them), THEN subtract.

4⅕ − 1 3/5 = ?  (⅕ is smaller than 3/5 — it doesn't fit!)

Step 1 — denominators already match (both 5). Check: is 1/5 ≥ 3/5? No — we can't subtract yet.
Step 2 — borrow 1 whole from the 4. That whole becomes 5/5 (five fifths, since our denominator is 5):
4⅕ = 3 wholes + 1 whole + 1/5 = 3 + 5/5 + 1/5 = 3 6/5
Step 3 — now subtract normally. Wholes:
3 − 1 = 2
Step 4 — fractions:
6/5 − 3/5 = 3/5
4⅕ − 1 3/5 = 2 3/5
Fraction to subtract is BIGGER than the fraction you have → borrow 1 whole = denominator/denominator extra pieces, THEN subtract.

📋 Watch A Whole Get Borrowed

Practice — Add & Subtract Mixed Numbers

P1.  3 2/7 + 1 4/7 = ?
Same denominator (7). Wholes: 3+1=4. Fractions: 2/7+4/7=6/7.
3 2/7 + 1 4/7 = 4&frac67;
P2.  2½ + 1 2/5 = ?
Common denominator of 2 and 5 is 10. 2½=2 5/10. 1 2/5=1 4/10.
Wholes: 2+1=3. Fractions: 5/10+4/10=9/10.
2½ + 1 2/5 = 3 9/10
P3.  5¼ − 2¾ = ?
1/4 < 3/4, so borrow: 5¼ = 4 5/4.
Wholes: 4−2=2. Fractions: 5/4−3/4=2/4=½.
5¼ − 2¾ = 2½
P4 (Word Problem).  Mia has 3 3/8 m of ribbon. She uses 1 5/8 m to tie a bow. How much ribbon does she have left?
3/8 < 5/8, so borrow: 3 3/8 = 2 11/8.
Wholes: 2−1=1. Fractions: 11/8−5/8=6/8=¾.
3 3/8 − 1 5/8 = 1¾ m of ribbon left
📖 Today's Big Idea

To add or subtract mixed numbers: match the denominators first, then combine the wholes and the fractions separately. When subtracting, if the fraction doesn't fit, borrow 1 whole and turn it into denominator-many extra fraction pieces.

🏆 You've got it when…

  • You can find a common denominator when the fractions don't match
  • You add/subtract wholes and fractions separately, then combine
  • You know WHEN you need to borrow (fraction to subtract is bigger than what you have)
  • You can borrow a whole correctly (it becomes denominator/denominator extra)
  • You always simplify your final answer

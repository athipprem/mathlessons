🏆 Why Learn This?

Where counting shows up — in real life and in contests:

How many games in a football league? How many ways to seat your friends? How many handshakes at a party? How many 4-digit lock codes are safe? Competition papers (like ASMOPSS) ask these constantly — and the numbers are too big to list one by one.

Today's superpower: count a big group without listing them all — and spot when a question is really a "choose 2" in disguise.

🔍 Building a thing? In a row? Or choosing a group?

✖️ BUILD — one from each group

Multiply the choices. 3 shirts × 2 hats = 6.

🔢 ARRANGE — in a row / 1st & 2nd

Count down: 5 × 4 × 3… Order matters, do NOT halve.

🤝 CHOOSE 2 — pairs (today's focus)

Order does NOT matter → n × (n−1) ÷ 2.

The Same Question in Many Costumes

pairs from n things = n × (n − 1) ÷ 2

These all sound different, but every one is "how many ways to choose 2 from n" — so they all use the same formula:

🤝 handshakes 📏 lines through points ⚽ league games (each pair once) 🎁 choosing a pair

The trick is to recognise the disguise — if two things join up and order doesn't matter, it's a pair.

Worked Example: 9 people each shake hands once with everyone. How many handshakes?

This is "choose 2 people" → use n × (n−1) ÷ 2 ← spot the pair
n = 9
9 × (9 − 1) = 9 × 8 ← each person shakes the other 8
9 × 8 = 72
72 ÷ 2 = 36 ← A–B is the same handshake as B–A
= 36 handshakes

Diagonals — Pairs Minus the Sides

A diagonal joins two corners but is NOT a side. So: count all pairs of corners, then subtract the sides.

Worked Example: how many diagonals does a hexagon (6 corners) have?

all pairs of corners = 6 × 5 ÷ 2 ← choose 2 corners
= 30 ÷ 2 = 15
subtract the 6 sides: 15 − 6 = 9 ← sides aren't diagonals
= 9 diagonals

⚠️ When NOT to halve

If order matters, each direction is its own thing — do NOT divide by 2.

8 friends each post a card to every other friend
A→B and B→A are TWO different cards ← order matters
8 × 7 = 56 cards ← no ÷ 2

💡 Quick test: "could you swap the two and it's the same thing?" Yes → halve (handshake). No → don't halve (a card, a race result, captain-and-keeper).

Making Numbers — Fill Each Slot

For "how many numbers/codes can be made", give each slot its own count and multiply. If digits can repeat, every slot has the full set of choices.

Worked Example: 2-digit numbers from 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 with repeats allowed.

tens slot: 5 choices
units slot: 5 choices ← repeats allowed, all 5 again
5 × 5 = 25
= 25 numbers

💡 If digits may NOT repeat, the next slot has one fewer: 5 × 4. Read which it is.

Practice

Pairs / handshakes → n×(n−1)÷2. Diagonals → pairs − sides. Order matters → don't halve.

Q1.   7 people at a meeting each shake hands once with every other person. How many handshakes?
choose 2 → 7 × 6 ÷ 2
7 × 6 = 42
42 ÷ 2 = 21
Q2.   There are 8 points on a page, no 3 in a line. How many straight lines join two points?
each line = a pair of points
8 × 7 ÷ 2 = 56 ÷ 2 = 28
Q3.   How many diagonals does a heptagon (a 7-sided shape) have?
all pairs of corners = 7 × 6 ÷ 2 = 21
subtract 7 sides: 21 − 7 = 14
Q4.   From 6 children, how many different pairs can be chosen to be line leaders (order does not matter)?
choose 2 → 6 × 5 ÷ 2
30 ÷ 2 = 15
📖 Counting on One Card
Build:one from each group → multiply. Arrange:in a row → count down (n×(n−1)×…). Choose 2:pairs / handshakes / lines / games → n×(n−1)÷2. Diagonals:pairs of corners − the sides. Don't halve when:order matters (cards, races, captain & keeper). Making numbers:fill each slot; repeats → full choices again.

🏆 You are ready when...

  • You spot "lines", "games", "handshakes" as the SAME choose-2 question
  • You always ask "could I swap them?" before deciding to ÷ 2
  • For diagonals you remember to subtract the sides

🏠 Real Grids Are Rarely Square

Why learn this?

A window is usually wider than it is tall. A bookshelf has more shelves than columns. A field of tiles is often a long rectangle, not a perfect square. Real life is full of grids where the width and height are different numbers — and that's exactly where the rectangle-counting rule gets tricky. On your last test, the rule worked fine on a square grid, but slipped on grids that were not square, and on longer strips. This lesson fixes that — one careful step at a time, every single time.

The rule NEVER changes. Only the numbers do. Width and height must be counted SEPARATELY.

The Rule, Said Very Precisely

Every rectangle in a grid is made by choosing 2 up-down lines (its left and right edges) and 2 across lines (its top and bottom edge).

Rectangles = (choose 2 of the up-down lines) × (choose 2 of the across lines)

The part Prem must slow down on: a grid that is w boxes wide has w + 1 up-down lines. A grid that is h boxes tall has h + 1 across lines. When w and h are different, these two counts are different numbers — you cannot use the same number for both.

⚠️ A "2×5 grid" means 2 boxes tall, 5 boxes wide. That is 3 across lines (2+1) but 6 up-down lines (5+1). Mixing these up is the single biggest cause of a wrong answer.

Worked Example, Part 1 — Draw & Count

How many rectangles of all sizes hide in a 2×3 grid (2 boxes tall, 3 boxes wide)?

Let's go really slowly. First, the one big idea — what a rectangle actually is:

Every rectangle needs 4 lines:
a LEFT wall and a RIGHT wall (2 up-down lines),
and a TOP and a BOTTOM (2 across lines).
Up-down lines: 1 2 3 4  ·  Across lines: A B C. The orange box is one rectangle: its walls are lines 2 & 4, its top & bottom are A & C.

See it? Pick any 2 up-down lines for the walls, and any 2 across lines for the top and bottom, and you have drawn exactly one rectangle. So the whole job is just two little "pick 2" jobs.

First, count the lines. (This is the step Prem must NEVER skip.)
3 boxes wide → up-down lines = 3 + 1 = 4  (lines 1, 2, 3, 4)
2 boxes tall → across lines = 2 + 1 = 3  (lines A, B, C)
4 and 3 are different numbers — that's normal, the grid isn't square. Keep them apart.

Now the two "pick 2" jobs — listed out

SHORTCUT FORMULA:  (n × (n − 1)) ÷ 2  —  where n = the number of lines you are choosing 2 from.

Always work out the bracket ( ) FIRST, then divide by 2. That way you multiply before you divide — and never get confused about which to do first.

Pick 2 of the 3 across lines (A, B, C). Just list every pair:
A&B ,   A&C ,   B&C  →  3 ways
Shortcut: (n × (n−1)) ÷ 2, where n = 3 lines. Bracket first:
(3 × 2) ÷ 2  =  6 ÷ 2  =  3
Pick 2 of the 4 up-down lines (1, 2, 3, 4). List every pair:
1&2 ,   1&3 ,   1&4 ,   2&3 ,   2&4 ,   3&4  →  6 ways
Shortcut: (n × (n−1)) ÷ 2, where n = 4 lines. Bracket first:
(4 × 3) ÷ 2  =  12 ÷ 2  =  6  — the SAME "pick 2" move as counting handshakes, each pair once.
Every top-&-bottom pair can team up with every left-&-right pair, so we multiply:
3 × 6 = 18 rectangles

🗺️ The Plan — Every Time, No Skipping

1Write down w (width in boxes) and h (height in boxes) separately. A 1-row strip has h = 1.
2Across lines = h + 1. Up-down lines = w + 1. Write both numbers down — do not do this in your head.
3Choose 2 of the across lines using (n × (n−1)) ÷ 2, where n = the number of lines (bracket first, then ÷2). Choose 2 of the up-down lines the same way.
4Multiply the two results. That is the total number of rectangles.
5Read the exact ask. If it wants rectangles that are NOT squares: also find the total SQUARES (count by size 1×1, 2×2, … and add), then subtract: rectangles − squares.

⚠️ Never eyeball a grid and guess the answer. Every single rectangle question gets the full 5-step working — even the "easy-looking" ones. That is exactly where the last test lost marks.

Worked Example — "NOT Squares"

In a 3×3 grid, how many rectangles are NOT squares?

Step 1 — total rectangles, using the 5-step plan:
w = 3, h = 3 → up-down lines = 4, across lines = 4
choose 2 of 4 (up-down): n=4 → (4 × 3) ÷ 2 = 6
choose 2 of 4 (across): n=4 → (4 × 3) ÷ 2 = 6
total rectangles = 6 × 6 = 36

Step 2 — Total Squares: Tap Each Size to SEE Where It Fits

A square is just a rectangle where both sides match. Watch how fewer and fewer spots are left as the square gets bigger.

☝ Tap a square size above

Explored: 1×1=?  +  2×2=?  +  3×3=?

✅ 9 + 4 + 1 = 14 total squares — that's the number Step 3 subtracts below!

1×1 squares = 9
2×2 squares = 4
3×3 squares = 1
total squares = 9 + 4 + 1 = 14
Step 3 — subtract:
36 − 14 = 22
22 rectangles that are NOT squares

⚠️ A square IS a rectangle — that is why we must subtract it out, not ignore it. Never invent a new shortcut for "NOT squares" — always reuse the two rules you already know: total rectangles, and total squares.

📖 Rectangles in Any Grid — in one line

Write w and h separately. Across lines = h+1, up-down lines = w+1. Choose 2 of each, multiply. "NOT squares" = rectangles − squares.

Never eyeball it — always write the two line-counts before multiplying, on every single question. And when unsure, check by counting sizes.

🏆 You've got it when…

  • You write w and h down first, every time, even on grids that "look easy"
  • You never use the same line-count for both directions unless w = h
  • You can turn "NOT squares" into two calculations you already know, then subtract

You now have the full rectangle toolkit — any grid, any size, and even "not squares".

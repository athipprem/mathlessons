🎯 The List Was Right. The Answer Wasn't.

Why learn this?

Imagine you count how many friends showed up to your party by writing their names: Ann, Ben, Cody, Dee, Emma. If someone asks "how many friends came?", the answer is 5 — not "Emma", the last name you wrote! On the last test, the LIST was built perfectly, every single time — but twice, the final answer written down was the wrong thing taken FROM that perfect list. This lesson is only about that very last step: turning a correct list into the correct number.

A perfect list is only half the job. The answer is HOW MANY things are in it — never the last thing you wrote.

Trap #2 — Does ORDER Matter?

Before you multiply anything, ask one question: does the order/job of each person or item matter?

Order does NOT matter (just a pair/team) → multiply, then ÷ 2 Order DOES matter (1st & 2nd, arranging in a row) → multiply, do NOT halve

Example A — "Choose 2 players from a team of 6 to be on the doubles pair" — a pair has no "1st" or "2nd", it's just a team. Order does NOT matter.

6 × 5 = 30 (pick a 1st player, then a 2nd)
But each pair got counted twice (A-then-B is the same team as B-then-A)
30 ÷ 2 = 15
15 pairs

Example B — "Choose a 1st place and 2nd place winner from 6 runners" — 1st and 2nd are different JOBS. Order DOES matter.

6 choices for 1st place
5 choices left for 2nd place
6 × 5 = 30
30 ways (do NOT halve — Alex-1st/Bo-2nd is different from Bo-1st/Alex-2nd)

🗺️ The Plan

1Build the list or count in a fixed order, writing every case.
2Before writing the final answer, re-read the question: does it ask "how many"? Then the answer is the COUNT of the list, never the last value in it.
3If the question involves choosing/pairing: ask "does order matter?" No → multiply then ÷2. Yes (1st/2nd, ranks, ordered picks) → multiply only.
4Sweep the FULL range — don't forget single-digit numbers if the range starts below 10.

⚠️ Two totally different mistakes, one fix each: (1) writing the last list-item instead of the count, and (2) using the wrong choose-2/order rule. Check BOTH every time you finish a listing question.

Worked Example — Both Traps Together

There are 5 books on a shelf. (a) How many different pairs of 2 books can you pick to donate? (b) How many 2-digit numbers have digits adding to 7?

(a) Donating a pair — order does NOT matter (no "1st book, 2nd book" job).
5 × 4 = 20
20 ÷ 2 = 10
10 pairs
(b) List in order, tens digit 1 up to 7:
16, 25, 34, 43, 52, 61, 70
Count the rows — do NOT write "70" as the answer:
1,2,3,4,5,6,7 → 7 rows
7 numbers

Your Turn

Build the list, then COUNT the rows (never copy the last value). For choosing: order matters → don't halve; order doesn't matter → halve.

P1.  How many 2-digit numbers have digits that add up to 9?
List: 18,27,36,45,54,63,72,81,90.
Count the rows: 9 numbers... wait check tens 1-9 all valid, units=9-tens≥0 always. That's 9 rows.
9 numbers (not 90 — that's the last item you listed)
P2.  How many different pairs of 2 friends can be chosen from a group of 4 friends?
A pair has no order → halve.
4 × 3 = 12. 12 ÷ 2 = 6.
6 pairs
P3.  Using the digits 1, 3 and 5, each exactly once, how many different 3-digit numbers can you make?
Every digit gets used, in a specific position → order matters, do NOT halve.
3 × 2 × 1 = 6.
6 numbers
P4.  How many different ways can you pay 15 cents using only 5-cent and 1-cent coins?
5c coins: 0,1,2,3 (0×5=0,1×5=5,2×5=10,3×5=15 — all ≤15, 1c fills the rest).
List: (0,15),(1,10),(2,5),(3,0) — 4 rows.
4 ways (not 15 — that's a coin total, not the count)
📖 Listing — in one line

The answer is the COUNT of your list, never the last value in it. Order matters → multiply only. Order doesn't matter → multiply then halve.

Your lists have been perfect all along — this lesson is just about the very last step.

🏆 You've got it when…

  • You count the rows of your list before writing the final answer
  • You ask "does order matter?" before deciding whether to halve
  • You double-check the full range for single-digit cases

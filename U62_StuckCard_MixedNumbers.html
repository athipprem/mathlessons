Before you read anything below — ask yourself:

"11 ÷ 4 — how many whole 4s fit inside 11, and what's left over?"

Say your answer out loud. Then scroll down and check.

(If you already know, you don't need the rest of this page — go answer the question!)

🧮 Go And Get 11 Things

Coins, Lego, buttons — anything you can count. Try this with "11 ÷ 4."

  1. Count out 11 things.
  2. Make piles of 4. Keep making piles of 4 until you don't have enough left for another full one.
  3. Count your FULL piles of 4: that's 2. Those are your whole pieces.
  4. Count what's left over (not enough for a full pile of 4): that's 3. That leftover becomes a fraction: 3 out of 4, or 3/4.
  5. Put it together: 2 whole piles, and 3/4 of another → 2 3/4.

Two Contrasted Examples, Side By Side

🔶 11 ÷ 4 — has a leftover
2 whole piles of 4 (that's the 8 used up)
3 left over (not enough for a 3rd pile)
= 2 3/4 (the leftover becomes a fraction)
✅ 12 ÷ 4 — no leftover
3 whole piles of 4 (exactly 12 used up)
0 left over (nothing to make a fraction from)
= 3 (just a whole number, no fraction needed)
If the division comes out exactly, the answer is a whole number. If it doesn't, the leftover becomes the top of a fraction — and what you divided by stays on the bottom.

⚠️ The single most common slip: writing the remainder and forgetting to put it OVER the number you divided by. "3 left over" on its own means nothing — it has to become 3/4.

Now You Try

1.  Write 15/4 as a mixed number.
2.  20 sweets are shared equally among 6 children. How many does each child get?

You've got it if you can say WHY the bottom number never changes — not just get the number.

🔤 Why Some "Different" Orders Look The Same

Why learn this?

You already know how to count arrangements with the factorial rule: 4 different toys can be lined up in 4! = 24 ways. But what if some of the items are IDENTICAL — like the letters in the word "DAD" (two D's that look exactly the same), or two identical twin runners in a race photo? Some of the n! orderings will look EXACTLY alike, so we can't count them as different. This shows up everywhere in competition problems: arranging letters in a word, arranging beads of the same colour, arranging people when some are "matching."

When items repeat, some arrangements look identical — and we must NOT count look-alikes twice.

The Big Idea — Divide Out The Look-Alikes

Remember the factorial rule: n different items in a row → n! arrangements. That rule assumes every item is DIFFERENT from every other. But if some items are identical twins (same letter, same colour, same object), swapping just those two twins with each other produces an arrangement that LOOKS exactly the same as before — so n! is now counting some arrangements more than once.

Count all n! orderings, then DIVIDE by the number of ways each repeated group could swap among itself (r!) — because those swaps don't create a new-looking arrangement.

⚠️ Only divide for items that are truly IDENTICAL (indistinguishable). Two different letters, even if they look similar, are NOT the same item.

Worked Example 1 — "DAD"

How many different-looking ways can the letters of "DAD" be arranged?

Step 1 — count total letters: D, A, D → n = 3 letters
Step 2 — find repeats: the letter D appears 2 times. A appears once (no division needed for A).
Step 3 — start with n! (as if every letter were different): 3! = 6
Step 4 — divide by r! for the repeated letter (r = how many times it repeats): divide by 2! = 2, because swapping the two D's with each other never changes what we SEE.
3! ÷ 2! = 6 ÷ 2 = 3
3 different-looking arrangements: ADD, DAD, DDA

Worked Example 2 — "BALL"

How many different-looking ways can the letters of "BALL" be arranged?

Total letters: B, A, L, L → n = 4
Repeats: L appears 2 times. B and A each appear once.
Start with n! = 4! = 24
Divide by r! for the repeated letter: 24 ÷ 2! = 24 ÷ 2 = 12
12 different-looking arrangements
Formula: n! ÷ (r₁! × r₂! × ...) — one r! for EACH letter that repeats.

🗺️ The Plan

1Count the total number of items/letters → that's n.
2Check for repeats. For EACH letter/item that repeats, count how many times it repeats (r).
3No repeats at all? The answer is just n!.
4Repeats found? Compute n! first, then divide by r! for EACH repeated letter (multiply the r!'s together if there's more than one repeated letter).
5Formula: n! ÷ (r₁! × r₂! × ...)

⚠️ A letter that appears 3 times means divide by 3! = 6, not by 3. Always turn the repeat COUNT into a factorial before dividing.

Your Turn

Count total letters (n), find each repeat count (r), then compute n! ÷ (r₁! × r₂! × ...).

P1.  How many different-looking arrangements of the letters in "MOON" are there?
n=4 (M,O,O,N). O repeats twice → divide by 2!
4! ÷ 2! = 24 ÷ 2 = 12
12
P2.  How many different-looking arrangements of the letters in "PUPPY" are there?
n=5 (P,U,P,P,Y). Look carefully — P appears 3 times (positions 1,3,4), not 2!
5! ÷ 3! = 120 ÷ 6 = 20
20
P3.  How many different-looking arrangements of the letters in "KITTEN" are there?
n=6 (K,I,T,T,E,N). T repeats twice → divide by 2!
6! ÷ 2! = 720 ÷ 2 = 360
360
P4.  How many different-looking arrangements of the letters in "PEPPER" are there?
n=6 (P,E,P,P,E,R). P repeats 3 times, E repeats 2 times → divide by 3! AND 2!
6! ÷ (3! × 2!) = 720 ÷ (6×2) = 720 ÷ 12 = 60
60
📖 Repeats Rule — in one line

n! ÷ (r₁! × r₂! × ...) — start with the full factorial, then divide out every repeated letter's own factorial.

This stops us from counting look-alike arrangements more than once.

🏆 You've got it when…

  • You count the TOTAL letters correctly for n
  • You spot EVERY repeated letter, not just the first one you notice
  • You turn each repeat count into a factorial (r!) before dividing

🎯 Why Learn This?

You already do this every day, without a name for it

"Will it rain today?" "Will I roll a six?" "Is my team likely to win?" Every one of these is really asking: how likely is this to happen? Weather forecasters, doctors, sports commentators, and game designers all use ONE tool to answer questions like that precisely, instead of just guessing — it's called probability.

Your new superpower

By the end of this lesson, you'll be able to turn "how likely is this?" into an exact number — a fraction — for any game, bag of marbles, spinner, or raffle. No more guessing "probably" or "maybe" — you'll be able to say exactly how likely, every time.

Probability = turning "how likely?" into an exact fraction.

Examples: "The sun will rise tomorrow" — CERTAIN. "You'll flip a fair coin and get heads" — EVEN CHANCE. "You'll roll a 6 on a single die" — UNLIKELY (possible, just not often). "A cat will grow wings" — IMPOSSIBLE.

⚠️ This scale is just words for now — the whole rest of this lesson is about turning these words into an EXACT number, so "unlikely" becomes something precise like "1 out of 6," not just a feeling.

🧮 Probability As A Fraction — The Core Rule

The one new idea

To turn "how likely?" into a number, you always ask TWO counting questions: how many outcomes do I want (favourable)? and how many outcomes are possible in total? Then you write favourable OVER total, as a fraction.

Probability of an event  =  (favourable outcomes) ÷ (total possible outcomes)

Example: A bag has 9 marbles: 4 green and 5 orange.

You reach in without looking and pull one marble. What's the probability it's GREEN?
Favourable outcomes (green marbles) = 4
Total possible outcomes (all marbles) = 9
P(green) = 4 ÷ 9 = 4/9

⚠️ That's it — probability is just a counting fraction. TOP = what you want. BOTTOM = everything that could happen.

⚖️ Equally Likely Outcomes

The one new idea

The favourable-over-total rule only works cleanly when every single outcome has the SAME chance of happening — we call this equally likely. A fair die, a fair coin, a spinner with equal-sized sections, and a bag where you can't feel the difference between marbles — all equally likely. Nothing is "sneaky" or weighted to happen more.

Fair die, fair coin, equal-sized spinner sections, a well-mixed bag → all outcomes equally likely.

Example: Rolling a fair 6-sided die — each of the 6 faces (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6) has exactly the same chance of landing face-up. That's why we can say "probability of rolling a 4" = 1/6 — there are 6 equally likely outcomes, and only 1 of them is a "4."

⚠️ Every probability question in this lesson (and the test) uses fair, equally-likely setups — dice, spinners with equal sections, and well-mixed bags. You never need to worry about one outcome being "more likely" than another unless a question tells you the bag/spinner is unfair.

🧭 Put It Together — The ONE Rule

P(event)  =  favourable outcomes ÷ total outcomes
1Count the TOTAL number of possible outcomes (everything that could happen).
2Count the FAVOURABLE outcomes (the ones that match what the question asks for).
3Write favourable ÷ total as a fraction.
4Check: can the fraction be SIMPLIFIED? If yes, simplify it (next screen shows how).

⚠️ Same 4 steps every single time, no matter what the question is about — marbles, spinners, dice, raffle tickets, anything.

8

✂️ Simplifying Probability Fractions

The one new idea

Sometimes favourable ÷ total gives a fraction that ISN'T in simplest form yet. You already know how to simplify fractions (divide top and bottom by the same number) — probability fractions follow that exact same rule. A probability answer is only finished once it's fully simplified.

Example: A box has 12 pencils: 8 blue and 4 red. What is P(red)?

Favourable (red) = 4. Total = 12.
P(red) = 4/12
Is 4/12 simplest? 4 and 12 are both divisible by 4:
4 ÷ 4 = 1,   12 ÷ 4 = 3
P(red) = 4/12 = 1/3
Always check: can top and bottom both be divided by the same number? If yes — keep dividing until you can't anymore.

⚠️ "1/3" and "4/12" mean the exact same chance — but when a question says "simplest form," only the fully-simplified fraction counts as the finished answer.

🚫 The "NOT" Rule

The one new idea

Sometimes a question asks for the probability that something does NOT happen. You already have everything you need — just count how many outcomes DON'T match, and put that over the same total.

Example: A jar has 16 buttons. 6 of them are broken. What is P(NOT broken)?

Step 1 — total outcomes:
16
Step 2 — favourable outcomes ("not broken" = the good ones):
16 − 6 = 10 good buttons
Step 3 — write as a fraction:
10/16
Step 4 — simplify (both divisible by 2):
P(not broken) = 10/16 = 5/8
"NOT" an event → favourable = total − (the thing you're avoiding). Same 4 steps as always.

Practice — Chance As A Fraction

P1.  A bag has 14 balls: 4 white and 10 black. What is P(white), in SIMPLEST form?
Favourable = 4, total = 14. P(white) = 4/14.
4/14 ÷ both by 2 = 2/7 (simplest form)
P2.  A raffle has 27 tickets. 3 of them are winners. What is P(NOT winning), in SIMPLEST form?
Not-winning = 27 − 3 = 24. P(not winning) = 24/27.
24/27 ÷ both by 3 = 8/9 (simplest form)
📖 Today's Big Idea

Probability = favourable outcomes ÷ total outcomes, for any equally-likely situation. Always simplify your fraction, and remember "NOT an event" = total minus the thing you're avoiding.

🏆 You've got it when…

  • You can count favourable and total outcomes from a picture or a word problem
  • You write probability as favourable ÷ total, every time
  • You always check if the fraction can be simplified — and simplify it fully
  • You can find P(NOT an event) using total minus the thing you're avoiding
  • You know "equally likely" means every outcome has the same chance

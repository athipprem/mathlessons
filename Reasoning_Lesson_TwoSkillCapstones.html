🔗 Why This Matters

The pattern in your hardest questions

Look back at M4 and M5's hardest tier (Round 3): every single miss was a "capstone" question — one that used TWO of your skills back to back, not one. Circular arrangements + repeats. Averages + removal. Listing + algebra. You know EVERY one of those skills separately, cold. What's new isn't the maths — it's recognizing that a question is asking for two moves, not one.

The good news

You don't need to learn anything new today. Every worked example in this lesson uses skills you've already passed a real test on. The only new move is: solve skill 1 all the way to a clean number, then use that number to start skill 2 — like handing a baton from one runner to the next.

Two skills, one question. Solve the FIRST one completely. Then feed that number into the SECOND one, like it's its own fresh question.

🧩 The Baton-Pass Method

1Read the WHOLE question first. Spot: is there really ONE ask here, or does solving it need two completely different tools?
2Name the two skills out loud (even just in your head) — e.g. "this is circular arrangements, THEN repeats."
3Solve Skill 1 on its own, exactly like it's a normal stand-alone question, until you have ONE clean number.
4Take that number and plug it straight into Skill 2 — solve Skill 2 exactly like ITS own stand-alone question too.
5Check: does your final answer only use ONE new number (the baton) passed from step 1 into step 2? If you needed information from nowhere, you skipped a clue.

That's the whole method. Four different skill-pairs are shown in this lesson so you can see the SAME baton-pass pattern working no matter which two skills are involved.

Pair 1 of 4 Circular Arrangements+ Repeats

Circular Arrangements + Repeats

7 friends sit around a circular table. Among them, 2 are wearing identical yellow hats (impossible to tell apart). The rest are all different. How many different arrangements are possible?

SKILL 1Circular arrangements of 7 people: (7−1)! = 6!
SKILL 22 of them are identical → divide by 2!
Step 1 (Skill 1 alone) — circular arrangements of 7: (7−1)! = 6! = 720
Step 2 (pass the baton: 720 → Skill 2) — divide by the repeat: 720 ÷ 2! = 720 ÷ 2 = 360
360 different arrangements

⚠️ Notice: Step 1 is the EXACT same circular-arrangements move from U42/U43. Step 2 is the EXACT same repeats-division move from U40/U41. Nothing new except doing them back to back.

Pair 2 of 4 Averages+ Removal

Averages + Removal

The average score across 4 games is 22 points. If a game scoring 10 points is removed, what is the new average of the remaining 3 games?

SKILL 1Find the TOTAL from the average: 4 × 22
SKILL 2Remove 10, then find the new average of 3 games
Step 1 (Skill 1 alone) — total of all 4 games: 4 × 22 = 88
Step 2 (pass the baton: 88 → Skill 2) — remove 10, divide by the new count: (88 − 10) ÷ 3 = 78 ÷ 3 = 26
The new average is 26

⚠️ Step 1 is the "total = average × count" move from U47. Step 2 is the "remove, then re-average" move from U47/U48. The baton is just the TOTAL number.

⭐ Your Turn

Practice — Averages + Removal

P1.  The average of 6 tests is 25. If a test scoring 15 is removed, what is the new average of the remaining 5 tests?
Skill 1: total = 6 × 25 = 150
Skill 2: (150−15) ÷ 5 = 135 ÷ 5 = 27
27
Pair 3 of 4 Counting+ Remainder

Counting/Listing + Remainder

Using the digits 2, 3, 5, 8, 9 (each digit used at most once), how many different 2-digit numbers can be formed? If that count is divided into groups of 7, how many are left over (the remainder)?

SKILL 1Count 2-digit numbers from 5 digits, no repeats (box method): 5 × 4
SKILL 2Divide that count by 7, find the remainder
Step 1 (Skill 1 alone) — first digit 5 choices, second digit 4 choices left: 5 × 4 = 20
Step 2 (pass the baton: 20 → Skill 2) — divide by 7: 20 ÷ 7 = 2 remainder 6  (7×2=14, 20−14=6)
The remainder is 6

⚠️ Step 1 is the box-method counting move from U4/U32. Step 2 is the systematic-remainder move from U34. The baton is the COUNT (20), used as the new "total" to divide.

⭐ Your Turn

Practice — Counting + Remainder

P1.  Using the digits 1, 4, 6, 8, 9 (each used at most once), how many different 2-digit numbers can be formed? If that count is divided into groups of 9, what is the remainder?
Skill 1: 5 × 4 = 20
Skill 2: 20 ÷ 9 = 2 remainder 2 (9×2=18, 20−18=2)
2
Pair 4 of 4 Counting (Pairs)+ Algebra

Counting Formula + Solve-Backward Algebra

At a party, every pair of guests shakes hands exactly once. There are 15 handshakes in total. How many guests are at the party?

SKILL 1Handshakes formula: n × (n−1) ÷ 2 = 15
SKILL 2Solve for n by testing consecutive numbers (like Unit 21's equation method)
Step 1 (Skill 1 alone) — write the formula as an equation: n × (n−1) = 15 × 2 = 30
Step 2 (pass the baton: 30 → Skill 2) — find two consecutive numbers that multiply to 30: 6 × 5 = 30 ✓
There are 6 guests

⚠️ This pair runs BACKWARD compared to the others — you're given the handshake TOTAL and have to work out n, instead of being given n and finding a total. The formula is the same one from U31; only the direction flips.

⭐ Your Turn

Practice — Handshake Formula, Solve Backward

P1.  At a club meeting, every pair of members shakes hands exactly once. There are 28 handshakes in total. How many members are there?
n × (n−1) = 28 × 2 = 56
8 × 7 = 56 ✓
8

🗺️ The Baton-Pass Checklist

1Read the whole question. Does it need TWO different tools, not one?
2Name both skills before you calculate anything.
3Solve Skill 1 completely, as if it were its own question, down to ONE clean number.
4Pass that ONE number into Skill 2. Solve Skill 2 exactly like a normal stand-alone question.
5Never try to do both skills in one messy step — that's when errors creep in.
6Some pairs run backward (you're given the END result and solve for the start) — check which direction the question is asking before you begin.

⚠️ If a question feels "too big to hold in your head," that's the signal it's a two-skill question — slow down and find the baton-pass point, don't try to rush both skills at once.

📖 Today's Big Idea

A "capstone" question isn't harder maths — it's two skills you already have, back to back. Solve Skill 1 completely to get ONE clean number, then pass that number into Skill 2 like it's a brand-new question.

🏆 You've got it when…

  • You can name BOTH skills in a question before calculating anything
  • You finish Skill 1 completely before touching Skill 2
  • You never try to blend two formulas into one messy step
  • You notice when a pair runs backward and adjust

This is exactly the skill that decided Round 3 on both M4 and M5. Every miss there was a two-skill question — and you already have every one of the skills involved.

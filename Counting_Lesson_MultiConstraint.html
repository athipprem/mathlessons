🎯 Why Learn This?

Real counting questions almost always have a catch

“Choose a team of 3 with at least 1 girl.”
“Pick 3 marbles that are not all the same colour.”
“Choose 3 points, but not ones that sit on a straight line.”

Counting the groups you WANT can be really painful. Counting the ones you DON’T want is often easy. So today you learn the lazy-but-clever way:

GOOD groups  =  ALL groups  −  BAD groups

💡 You will use yesterday’s Move TEAM (multiply 3, divide by 6) twice in every question — once for ALL, once for BAD. Nothing else is new except the subtraction.

🤝 You Already Know This Trick

In Unit 55 (Probability) you learned the NOT rule — and you got it right on the test. Instead of working out the messy thing directly, you found the easy opposite and took it away:

What you already know (Probability):
P(NOT rain) = 1 − P(rain)
Today’s new version (Counting):
Count(GOOD) = Count(ALL) − Count(BAD)

⚠️ Only one difference. In probability the whole thing was 1 (one whole). In counting the whole thing is the total number of groups. Same thinking, different starting number.

🔍 Spotting The Signal

Watch for these words in the question

“at least 1…” · “not all the same…” · “excluding…” · “but NOT…”

Each one describes the good groups as “everything except some annoying exception.” That is your signal to flip it around.

ALL groups
−
BAD groups
(the exception)
=
GOOD groups
(the answer)

⚠️ Why bother? “At least 1 girl” means 1 girl OR 2 girls OR 3 girls — three separate counts, slow and easy to mess up. But “0 girls” is just ONE quick count. Flipping turns three jobs into one.

Worked Example 1 — At Least 1 Girl

A class has 4 boys and 3 girls (7 students in total). A group of 3 students is chosen to present a project. How many different groups contain AT LEAST 1 girl?

B
B
B
B
G
G
G
Step 1 — count ALL groups of 3, ignoring the extra rule completely. Choose 3 from all 7:
7 × 6 = 42
42 × 5 = 210
210 ÷ 6 = 35 groups altogether
Step 2 — what does BAD mean? “At least 1 girl” is broken only by having ZERO girls — that is, all 3 are boys. Choose 3 from the 4 boys:
4 × 3 = 12
12 × 2 = 24
24 ÷ 6 = 4 bad groups
Step 3 — take the bad ones away:
35 − 4 = 31 groups with at least 1 girl
All 35 groups, split into good and bad:
31 GOOD (at least 1 girl)
4 BAD

⚠️ The classic mistake: thinking the opposite of “at least 1 girl” is “all girls.” It is not. Always ask: what is the ONE situation that breaks the rule? Here it is having no girls at all.

Worked Example 2 — Cutting Out A Bad Case

9 points are marked on a page. Exactly 3 of them lie on one straight line (shown in red). No other 3 points are in a straight line. How many different triangles can be made by joining 3 of the 9 points?

Step 1 — ALL: choose any 3 of the 9 points, ignoring the straight-line problem:
9 × 8 = 72
72 × 7 = 504
504 ÷ 6 = 84 groups of 3 points
Step 2 — BAD: a group fails only if all 3 chosen points are the 3 red ones (a flat line is not a triangle). How many ways to choose those 3 red points? There is only one way — take all 3 of them:
1 bad group
Step 3 — subtract:
84 − 1 = 83 different triangles

⚠️ Check it with the rule if you like: choosing 3 from 3 is 3 × 2 × 1 ÷ 6 = 6 ÷ 6 = 1. It agrees — there really is only one way to pick all 3 of them.

🧩 When BAD Comes In More Than One Piece

So far, BAD was one single count. Sometimes the rule can be broken in several different ways. Then you count each way, and add them up before subtracting.

GOOD = ALL − (bad way 1 + bad way 2 + bad way 3 + …)

Worked Example 3 — Not All The Same Colour

A bag holds 4 red, 4 blue and 2 yellow counters (10 in total). Three counters are taken out. How many different groups are NOT all the same colour?

Step 1 — ALL groups of 3 from the 10 counters:
10 × 9 = 90, then 90 × 8 = 720
720 ÷ 6 = 120 groups altogether
Step 2 — list EVERY way to be bad. “All the same colour” can happen 3 different ways — all red, all blue, or all yellow. Count each one on its own:
All red: choose 3 from 4 → 4 × 3 × 2 ÷ 6 = 4
All blue: choose 3 from 4 → 4 × 3 × 2 ÷ 6 = 4
All yellow: choose 3 from 2 → there are only 2 yellow counters, so this is IMPOSSIBLE → 0
Step 3 — add the bad ways together:
4 + 4 + 0 = 8 bad groups
Step 4 — subtract:
120 − 8 = 112 groups that are not all the same colour

⚠️ Pretend every counter is different. Even though the 4 red ones look identical, treat them as if each has a tiny secret number on it (red-1, red-2, red-3, red-4). That is what makes “choose 3 from 4 reds = 4 ways” correct.

0️⃣ The Zero Case — Don’t Be Fooled

That yellow line above is worth its own page, because it looks strange the first time.

If there are fewer than 3 to choose from,
the number of groups of 3 is 0 — not 1, not “impossible”.

Writing 0 is important: you still list that bad case in your working, you just record it as zero. That way you never forget to check it, and you never accidentally subtract something that was never there.

The rule agrees. Choose 3 from 2:
2 × 1 × 0 = 0
0 ÷ 6 = 0 ways

⚠️ Notice the third number in “2 × 1 × 0”. You count DOWN three numbers from 2, so you land on 0 — and anything times 0 is 0. The maths is telling you “you ran out of counters.”

🧭 The Total Minus Bad Roadmap

1Spot the signal words: “at least 1”, “not all the same”, “excluding”, “but not”.
2Count ALL — choose 3 from the whole pool, ignoring the extra rule completely.
3List EVERY way to be bad. Sometimes there is one; sometimes there are two or three. Write them all down before you count anything.
4Count each bad way with the same choose-3 method. If a colour or group has fewer than 3 members, write 0 — do not skip it.
5Add the bad counts together, then subtract from ALL.
6Check you answered the exact question — it wants the GOOD number, not the ALL number and not the BAD number.
GOOD = ALL − (bad 1 + bad 2 + bad 3 + …)

📋 Build The Count Yourself

A bag has 5 red counters and 4 blue counters (9 in total). Three counters are drawn. How many different groups of 3 contain AT LEAST 1 blue counter?

Do each step yourself first, then tap to check.

9 × 8 = 72, then 72 × 7 = 504, then 504 ÷ 6 = 84
There is only one way to break “at least 1 blue”: get 0 blue, meaning all 3 are red.
5 × 4 = 20, then 20 × 3 = 60, then 60 ÷ 6 = 10
84 − 10 = 74 groups with at least 1 blue counter. (The question asked for GOOD — not 84, not 10.)
⭐ Your Turn

Practice — 4 Questions

🚦 For every question: write ALL first, then list EVERY bad way (some are 0), add them, subtract. Then check you wrote the GOOD number.

P1.  A basket has 8 red apples and 3 green apples (11 in total). Three apples are picked. How many different groups contain AT LEAST 1 green apple?
ALL: 11 × 10 × 9 = 990, then 990 ÷ 6 = 165
BAD (0 green = all 3 red): 8 × 7 × 6 = 336, then 336 ÷ 6 = 56
165 − 56 = 109 groups with at least 1 green apple
A) 165 is ALL and B) 56 is BAD — both are half-finished answers.
P2.  A club has 6 boys and 4 girls (10 members). Three members are chosen for a quiz team. How many different teams contain AT LEAST 1 boy?
ALL: 10 × 9 × 8 = 720, then 720 ÷ 6 = 120
BAD (0 boys = all 3 girls, from 4 girls): 4 × 3 × 2 = 24, then 24 ÷ 6 = 4
120 − 4 = 116 teams with at least 1 boy
P3.  A jar has 2 black, 3 white and 4 grey marbles (9 in total). Three marbles are drawn. How many different groups are NOT all the same colour?
ALL: 9 × 8 × 7 = 504, then 504 ÷ 6 = 84
Three ways to be bad — count each one:
All black: only 2 black exist, and you need 3 → 0
All white: 3 × 2 × 1 = 6, then 6 ÷ 6 = 1
All grey: 4 × 3 × 2 = 24, then 24 ÷ 6 = 4
BAD altogether: 0 + 1 + 4 = 5
84 − 5 = 79 groups that are not all the same colour
P4.  12 points are marked on a page. Exactly 4 of them lie on one straight line. No other 3 points are in a straight line. How many different triangles can be made by joining 3 of the 12 points?
ALL: 12 × 11 × 10 = 1320, then 1320 ÷ 6 = 220
BAD = any 3 chosen from the 4 points sitting on the line — they make a flat line, not a triangle. Careful: this time it is NOT just 1 bad group, because there are 4 points to choose 3 from.
4 × 3 × 2 = 24, then 24 ÷ 6 = 4 bad groups
220 − 4 = 216 different triangles
C) 220 forgets to subtract. A) 219 assumes only 1 bad group, like the 3-point example — but with 4 points on the line there are 4 ways to pick 3 of them.
📖 Today’s Big Idea

When the question says “at least 1”, “not all the same” or “excluding” — don’t chase the hard case. Count ALL, list every BAD way (writing 0 where a case is impossible), add the bad ones up, and subtract.

GOOD = ALL − (bad 1 + bad 2 + bad 3 + …)

🚦 Finish-Line Check — the last number you worked out is usually NOT the answer. The answer is the GOOD number, after subtracting. Read the question one more time before you write it down.

🏆 You’ve got it when…

  • You spot the signal words and flip to Total Minus Bad without being told
  • You know the opposite of “at least 1 girl” is “0 girls” — not “all girls”
  • You always LIST the bad ways before counting any of them
  • You write 0 for an impossible bad case instead of skipping it
  • You subtract at the end, and hand in the GOOD number
